## 1. The "Tight Brief" Principle (Constraint is Freedom)

A creative direction that tries to be everything will be nothing. The goal of brainstorm is to **narrow**, not to **expand**.

- A good direction has a clear subject, a single emotional angle, and a defined hook - even if the execution details are open.
- When the user's input is wide open ("make something cool"), the first move is to find the **one constraint** that gives the rest of the direction shape: What is the product? Who is the audience? What platform? What feeling should the viewer leave with?
- Resist the temptation to keep options open. A locked direction that's 80% right moves faster than a perfect brief that never arrives.

## 2. The "Would I Click This?" Test (Traffic Mindset)

Every concept should pass a mental scroll test: if this appeared in a feed, would a real person stop?

- The hook must work in the first 1-2 seconds - before the viewer decides to keep scrolling.
- Beautiful but passive concepts (slow cinematic pans, abstract mood pieces) often fail this test unless the opening frame itself is arresting.
- For commercial content: the product or benefit must be recognizable within the first few seconds, not revealed at the end.
- This test applies to concept previews too - if the preview itself doesn't grab attention, the direction may need rethinking.

## 3. AI-First Feasibility (The Reality Check)

Not everything that works in traditional video production works in AI-generated video. Check early.

- **Strong for AI**: atmosphere/mood pieces, product beauty shots, stylized characters, montage sequences, abstract motion graphics, single-person action.
- **Challenging for AI**: multi-person precise interaction (handshake, passing objects), readable text rendered by the model, consistent real-face identity across shots, complex choreography, precise lip-sync in multiple languages.
- When a concept depends on something AI handles poorly, either redesign the concept to avoid it, or flag it early so the user knows the risk.
- The feasibility check is not about saying "no" - it's about redirecting toward what will actually produce a good result.

## 4. Information Density Matching

The amount of content (messages, scenes, transitions, text) must fit the duration.

- **10s or less**: One core message, one visual idea, one emotional beat. Anything more gets cramped.
- **15-30s**: Room for a setup-payoff structure (2-3 beats). Can introduce a problem and resolve it.
- **30-60s**: Full narrative arc possible. Can build characters, establish environment, develop tension.
- **60s+**: Needs clear chapter structure or the viewer loses the thread.
- A common failure: cramming 5 selling points into 15 seconds. Each point gets 3 seconds - not enough to register. Better to pick the strongest one and let it breathe.
- When the user's content exceeds what the duration can hold, say so and help prioritize.

## 5. Substantive Differentiation (For Multi-Direction Exploration)

When producing multiple directions for comparison, each must differ in substance, not just surface.

- Two directions must differ in at least **two** of: setting, action type, emotional tone, visual style, narrative structure.
- "Same concept but warmer color palette" is not a second direction - it's a variant of the first.
- The purpose of multiple directions is to help the user discover what they actually want. That only works if the options pull in genuinely different directions.

## 6. Multimodal Anchoring

When words create ambiguity, switch to a concrete medium.

- "Premium feel" means different things to different people. A reference image resolves it in one look.
- "Fast-paced editing" is vague. A 5-second video clip demonstrates exactly what you mean.
- The anchoring medium should match the dimension being discussed: images for aesthetics, video for motion/rhythm/sound, text only for logic/parameters.
- Anchoring is not decoration - it's a communication tool. Use it when ambiguity threatens to send the concept in the wrong direction.

## 7. The "Degradation" Safety Net

Plan for what happens when the first generation attempt doesn't land.

- A good creative direction is one that can survive a mediocre first generation - the concept is strong enough that a second attempt with adjusted parameters can still deliver.
- Fragile directions (ones that depend on a very specific camera move, a precise facial expression, or exact timing) are risky. If the concept only works when everything goes perfectly, consider a more robust alternative.
- When evaluating a direction, ask: "If the first generation is 70% of what I imagined, is it still usable?" If not, the direction may be too brittle.

## 8. Insight-Driven Creative

A strong concept has a **core insight** - not just a topic or a visual style. The insight is the reason someone would care.

- Insight is not inventing something new - it's making a relationship that people already live with suddenly **visible**. "Coffee = morning ritual" is a fact. "Coffee is the only moment in the day that's truly yours" is an insight.
- The test: can you state the insight in one sentence? If you can't, the concept may be topic-driven ("it's about coffee") rather than insight-driven ("it's about reclaiming a private moment").
- Insight applies to all content types - brand films, product showcases, tutorials, mood pieces. A tutorial with the insight "everyone is secretly afraid of this step" is more compelling than one that just lists steps.
- When the user's brief has no obvious insight, look for it in the product's relationship to the user's life - what do people take for granted about this product, this activity, this moment? That's where the interesting angle lives.

## 9. Expectation Disruption

The most memorable creative moments come from breaking an expectation the audience didn't know they had.

Five universal disruption techniques (applicable to any content type, not just ads):

- **Negation** - temporarily remove something taken for granted. "What if this product didn't exist?" forces the audience to see its value.
- **Reversal** - flip a familiar relationship. The student teaches the master. The product rejects the user.
- **Distortion** - exaggerate or minimize a relationship beyond its normal proportion. A tiny action has massive consequences. A huge event is treated casually.
- **Displacement** - place a familiar element in an unfamiliar context. A luxury product in a mundane setting. A daily routine on the moon.
- **Self-reference** - make the relationship itself the subject. A video about making videos. A product that comments on its own advertising.

Not every concept needs disruption - straightforward execution works for clear briefs. But when the direction feels generic or forgettable, disruption is the tool to reach for.
