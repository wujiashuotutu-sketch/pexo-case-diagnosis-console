# Content

## 1. Scope

This guide covers **on-screen speech** only:
- **Dialogue**: character-to-character spoken exchange on screen
- **Monologue**: a single on-screen character speaking aloud or in a clearly staged internal-speech moment

It does **not** cover off-screen narration. Off-screen narration belongs to `vo-design-principles.md` and is **normally** treated as post-production voiceover, with a narrow exception for true one-shot final deliveries where narration may be co-generated with the single delivered segment.

## 2. Narrative Function

### Dialogue
- Best for relationship revelation, conflict, negotiation, teasing, or call-and-response energy
- Gives the audience character dynamics quickly without extra exposition
- Works best when each line changes the scene state: decision made, attitude exposed, tension raised, joke landed

### Monologue
- Best for self-disclosure, hesitation, realization, determination, or reflective emotional beats
- Works when the audience should feel close to one character's inner state while still watching performance on screen
- Most effective when the spoken line and the visible action create a slight tension or echo, not pure duplication

## 3. Writing Principles

### Dialogue Writing
- Keep lines short and playable; most short-form scenes only need 1-2 strong lines per beat
- Let speakers sound distinct in intent: one pushes, one resists; one jokes, one doubts
- Prefer implied information over explanatory lines
- End on a line that changes momentum, not a flat summary

### Monologue Writing
- Write as spoken thought, not essay prose
- Use incomplete thought, hesitation, repetition, or self-correction when it helps naturalism
- Anchor the line to a visible trigger: an object touched, a look exchanged, a decision made
- Keep emotional direction narrow within a short beat; abrupt emotional swings break plausibility

## 4. Image-Speech Relationship

### Direct Reveal
The line says what the visual action is doing. Useful for clarity, comedy, tutorial-like moments.

### Counterpoint
The line and the image pull in different emotional directions. Useful for irony, emotional complexity, denial.

### Pressure Build
Speech adds urgency while visuals keep moving toward a payoff. Useful for countdowns, pursuit, argument escalation.

### Quiet Interior
Minimal monologue over restrained action. Useful for realization beats, memory fragments, intimate character moments.

## 5. Cross-Segment Planning Rule

Dialogue and monologue may be designed for co-generation only when they are **in-sequence eligible**:
- The speaking beat starts and ends within one segment
- The emotional register stays narrow inside that beat
- The same character remains visually anchored
- The line is short enough to resolve naturally within the shot plan

Do **not** plan co-generated dialogue/monologue when:
- A sentence must continue across the cut
- One long speech carries the whole scene
- The emotional delivery needs deterministic identity across multiple segments
- The speech belongs to an off-screen narrator rather than an on-screen speaker

When a spoken idea must span multiple segments, convert it to **post voiceover** or rewrite it into separate self-contained in-scene beats.

## 6. Practical Creative Planning Guidance

**Describe dialogue qualitatively**, e.g.:
- "two friends trading dry, quick lines with a teasing rhythm"
- "a tense low-volume exchange, each line clipped and guarded"

**Describe monologue qualitatively**, e.g.:
- "a half-whispered self-check before she commits to the action"
- "a calm reflective line that lands after the movement slows"

**In the creative script**, include:
- who speaks
- why this line exists dramatically
- whether it is dialogue or monologue
- whether it is in-sequence eligible for co-generation or must be converted to post voice treatment

**Avoid**:
- treating dialogue/monologue as generic "voice"
- writing long explanatory copy better suited to narration
- splitting one sentence across segment boundaries just to match timing

**Focus**:
- on-screen playability
- beat-level dramatic function
- short self-contained speech units that match visible action
